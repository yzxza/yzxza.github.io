用大模型写受控医学文档，难点不在"写得通顺"，而在"错了能不能被发现"。这篇记录我在一个知情同意书（ICF）智能撰写项目里的思考：为什么要先治理模板、系统怎么设计、输出怎么让人核对，以及我踩过的坑。When LLMs draft controlled medical documents, the hard part is not fluent writing but whether mistakes can be caught. This retrospective covers an AI project for drafting informed consent forms (ICFs): why the template had to be governed first, how the system was designed, how the output was made easy to review, and the pitfalls along the way.

本文为个人项目复盘，已做脱敏处理：不涉及任何公司、项目编号、药物、研究方案或人员信息。文中的例子均为通用示意。

写在前面

项目是用 AI 起草临床试验的知情同意书（ICF）。

做之前我以为这是个”让大模型照着模板写一份文档”的问题。做下来发现，真正的难点完全不在”写”，而在三件事：

  1. **AI 拿不准的时候，它会不会老实说”不知道”**，而不是编一句看起来很对的话；
  2. 审稿的人能不能快速核对，而不是从头逐字再读一遍；
  3. 模板一升级，系统能不能跟着变。

这篇文章按”问题 → 判断 → 解法 → 交付 → 我做了什么 → 反思”的顺序展开。如果你只有两分钟，看第二节和第七节就够了。

一、ICF 是什么，为什么难写

知情同意书是患者参加临床试验前必须读完并签字的文件。没有签字，任何试验检查都不能开始；医院伦理委员会不批准它，研究就不能入组。

可以这样理解它和研究方案的关系：

  • 研究方案是写给医生和监管看的”技术说明书”，几百页，满是专业术语；
  • ICF 只挑出其中和患者有关的部分，用大白话告诉他：要做什么检查、有什么风险、有什么好处、花不花钱、隐私怎么保护、随时可以退出。

一份 ICF 通常有十几个章节，每章回答患者的一个问题：

章节 患者的问题 内容来源
关于研究 / 研究药物 研究什么药？怎么分组？ 研究方案、研究者手册
研究期间会发生什么 每次来医院做什么？ 方案里的访视流程表
风险与不适 / 妊娠避孕 有什么风险？要避孕多久？ 药物警戒部门的标准文字、方案
益处 / 替代治疗 参加有什么好处？不参加还能怎样？ 方案、临床团队
费用、隐私、样本 花不花钱？数据和样本去哪？ 预算、中心资料、法规要求
退出、联系人、签字页 能退出吗？有问题找谁？ 固定文字、各医院信息

起草一份 ICF，通常是临床运营的同事对着几百页方案和一份几十页的公司模板，逐章”删选项、填空、改写成白话”，再交给药物警戒、医学、法务等多个部门往返审核。

它难在四个地方：

  • 信息分散：访视、检查项目、避孕时长、禁用药散落在方案各处，很多还藏在大表格的脚注里；
  • 选项多：模板里同一段经常有好几种写法（比如研究目的就分 I 期、对照药、背景治疗等写法），要按研究设计选对；
  • 要翻译成白话：方案是专业术语，患者要看得懂；
  • 写错有合规风险：没有依据写”已获批准”、改动了风险标准文字、漏告知数据出境——这些都可能导致伦理退回。

前三个是 AI 擅长的；第四个恰恰是 AI 最危险的地方。整个产品设计，就是在发挥前者、防住后者。

二、第一个关键判断：这不是”写作”问题

项目初期最直接的思路是：把方案和模板一起丢给大模型，让它”照着模板写一份”。

这样写出来的稿子读起来很通顺，但问题全藏在看不见的地方：

  1. 该删的没删，不该删的删了。 很多公司模板用字体颜色区分”能不能改”（比如黑字不能动、蓝字可改），但蓝字里混着待填内容、写作提示、选项标签和候选正文。人一看就懂，模型分不清。
  2. 拿不准时会编。 找不到批件号、补贴金额、避孕时长，模型倾向于填一个”像样”的值。
  3. 固定文字被润色。 法规要求一字不改的条款，模型会顺手改得更”流畅”。
  4. 排版保不住。 页脚版本号、编号、签字页不跨页这些要求，模型直接改 Word 很难稳定。
  5. 审稿人无法核对。 不知道哪句来自模板、哪句是 AI 写的、依据在方案哪一页，只能从头读一遍——省下的时间又花回去了。

所以我对这个问题的定义变成了：

ICF 撰写不是开放式写作，而是”在受控模板上做合规判断 + 从方案里取证填空”。

这个定义决定了后面所有的设计：不让 AI 自由发挥，而是让它在一套明确的规则里做选择题和填空题。

三、解法：先治理模板，再谈生成

既然模板里的规则是”靠人理解的暗规则”，那就先把它们写成明确的、机器能执行的规则。我们把这一步叫”模板治理”，具体做了三件事。

1. 给模板的每一块钉上”门牌号”

把整份模板切成约 190 个小块，每块用 Word 的内容控件（一个带名字的框）圈起来，名字按”章 → 段 → 选项”分层，比如：

切块的原则是：一个能单独判断”留不留”的意思，就是一块。 比如男性避孕部分会拆成”对精子的影响””避孕要求””禁止捐精””输精管结扎豁免””伴侣怀孕”五块，因为不同研究可能只需要其中几块。

2. 给每一块写一条处理规则

规则以批注的形式挂在每个框上。归纳下来只有四类：

规则类型 白话意思 例子
固定保留 一字不动 签字页、研究伤害条款
多选一 几个候选只留一个，每个候选写明适用条件 研究目的的四种写法
看条件保留 满足条件才留；判断不了就留，并说明原因 “研究包含 MRI 检查”才保留 MRI 风险说明
按实际数量重复 模板只给一个样例，按方案实际有几个就写几个 治疗组、访视清单

3. 在块里标出填空和写作提示

  • [字段名] 是待填的空。从方案里找得到就填；找不到就原样留着，让审稿人一眼看到缺什么。
  • {…} 是写给 AI 的提示，生成后删除。

这些提示里沉淀了大量业务经验，几个典型：

  • 找不到批件号时，不能写”已获批准”，也不能用伦理批准代替监管批准；
  • 非肿瘤研究不能出现”后续抗肿瘤治疗”这类肿瘤专用说法；
  • 单盲研究不能沿用双盲的说明；
  • 药物风险只能用药物警戒部门提供的标准文字，AI 不能自己写；但方案里已经写明的事实（比如哪次访视验孕），不能以”需要药物警戒确认”为由留空。

一个完整例子——“盲法说明”这一块：

部分 内容
正文 “您和您的研究医生都无法预先知道您被分配到的组别……”
规则 看条件保留：方案是双盲才保留；判断不了就保留并说明原因
提示 单盲或其他盲法不能直接用这段，要按方案另写

AI 读完方案后只会有三种结果：双盲 → 保留；开放标签 → 删除；方案没写清 → **保留并标为”待确认”**。全部约 190 块都是这样处理的。

四、系统怎么跑：AI 交”答题卡”，程序负责排版和检查

我常用一个类比向业务同事解释：

  • 模板 = 试卷：约 190 道题，每道题附着答题规则；
  • AI = 考生：读完方案，对每道题交一个答案——留不留、选哪个、填什么、依据在方案哪里、拿不准的原因；
  • 程序 = 阅卷机 + 排版员：先检查答题卡有没有漏题、格式对不对，再按编号把答案填回模板，生成 Word。

完整流程是这样的：

  1. 上传研究方案，选择模板；
  2. 系统把方案解析成结构化文本；
  3. 分三组同时起草：封面到研究流程一组，风险到费用一组，隐私到签字页一组；每组写完先自查；
  4. 三组结果合并，再做一次全文检查；
  5. 程序按门牌号把答案填回模板，输出 Word 草稿。

这里有两个设计取舍：

做法 好处 代价
按章节分组同时写 每组只看一部分，更专注、更快；某组出错只重跑那组 组与组之间可能前后不一致，必须靠合并后的全文检查兜底
AI 只交答案，不直接改 Word 格式由程序保证；答案是结构化的，能自动检查有没有漏 要维护一套答案格式和检查规则

还有一条贯穿始终的原则：拿不准就保留，并写明原因。 因为两种错误的代价完全不对称——多留一段，审稿人几秒钟删掉；少告知一项风险，就是合规问题。

五、输出设计：让审稿人知道先看哪里

做到这里我意识到，**这类 AI 产品真正的交付物不是”一份终稿”，而是”一份能被快速核对的草稿”**。所以输出花了不少心思。

对话里给一段结果说明，包括三样东西：

  1. AI 对研究的关键判断，比如”多中心、单臂、开放的 III 期研究”。这一句要最先核对——它判断错了，后面很多块的取舍都会跟着错。
  2. 还需要补充的信息类别：批件号、各医院的研究者和联系人、药物风险标准文字、补贴金额、样本和跨境细节等。
  3. 一组统计，本质上是一份核对工作量清单：
类别 用户要不要管
放心保留的区块（多为固定文字） 一般不用
需关注的保留区块 / 控制区块 要看
已删除的区块 要抓紧看——删错了就是漏告知
待补字段 要补

Word 草稿里有三层核对线索：

  • 修订痕迹：AI 删掉的内容不是直接消失，而是以删除线显示，审稿人同意就”接受”，不同意就”拒绝”；
  • 高亮：标出需要留意的内容和待填字段；
  • 批注：每一块都有一条固定格式的说明——处理动作、对应哪一块、触发了哪条规则、判断依据或拿不准的原因、还缺哪些字段。

我们建议的核对顺序是：先核对研究类型判断 → 再看删除线 → 再处理需关注区块 → 最后补字段。 风险从高到低。

六、我在项目里做的几件事

接手这个项目后，我主要做了这几件事：

1. 把规则体系”读懂”并梳理成业务能看懂的语言。 逐块读完模板里的规则和提示，归纳出四类规则和它们的分布，写成一份不需要技术背景也能看懂的项目说明，让业务、药物警戒和开发能用同一套语言讨论问题。

2. 发现并定位了”模板版本没对齐”的问题。 公司模板在项目进行中升级了一版。我把新旧两版逐段比对，发现章节结构没变，真正的变化可以归成三类：

  • 用词规范化（只改字）；
  • 新增”个人信息和生物样本传到境外”的告知段落（只在研究涉及境外传输时保留）；
  • 新增”需要另外生成配套文件”的提醒（如怀孕伴侣授权书、个人信息处理告知书）。

而系统底稿仍是旧版——第一类审稿时会被挑出来，第二类对涉及境外传输的项目来说是实打实的合规缺口。由此我推动把”模板版本 → 规则版本 → 底稿版本”绑定起来：模板一升版，必须先更新规则、再更新底稿、再用一个对应场景的项目试跑验证。

3. 诊断”待确认太多”。 一份草稿往往有几十处需要人确认。我把它拆成两类：

  • 真的缺信息：方案里本来就没有（如各医院联系人、补贴金额），解法是增加补充资料的上传入口，并按负责人（药物警戒 / 法务 / 预算 / 中心）分组派发；
  • 方案里有、AI 没找到：这是要优化的部分，需要靠评测定位到具体是哪类信息、哪种表格解析得不好。

4. 设计评测方案。 程序检查”0 错误”只说明答题卡填完整了，不说明答对了。我设计的评测是：

  • 用已定稿、已过伦理的 ICF 做金标准，覆盖 I 期 / III 期、肿瘤 / 非肿瘤、双盲 / 开放、健康人 / 患者；
  • 把定稿 ICF 逐块映射回模板，再和 AI 的答案比；
  • 看几个数：留删判断准确率、填空召回率和准确率、访视覆盖率；外加两条红线：编造数为 0、标准文字改动数为 0；
  • 人工审稿不打 1–5 分，而是每块回答三个问题：事实对吗？该不该在？看得懂吗？这样问题能直接定位到某一块的规则、提示词或解析。

5. 体验上的去技术化。 过程卡片里原来是提示词、脚本命令和文件路径，批注里是”COVER/L4””always”这类内部写法。对业务用户来说，这些都应该换成”第 1/3 组已完成，0 错误，12 项待确认”这样的结果语言。

七、几个取舍和反思

1. 规则放在哪里：Word 模板里，还是代码和提示词里？
我们选了 Word 模板（以批注形式）。代价是开发改起来没那么方便；好处是业务方看得懂、能参与维护。对受控文档来说，规则的”所有权”应该在业务方，这一点比开发效率更重要。

2. 拿不准时，留还是删？
选”留 + 说明原因”。代价是待确认项多，所以清单体验必须做好——这是一个设计选择带出来的必然后续工作。

3. 按章节拆，还是按事实类型拆？
项目里讨论过两种架构：一种按章节分组并行（快、实现简单）；另一种先把跨章节共用的事实（当前研究药物、关键设计决定、完整访视矩阵、风险）抽成几份”事实底稿”，再统一写进各章（一致性更好、但更慢）。目前用的是前者，靠全文检查兜底一致性。我的判断是：当跨章节矛盾成为主要错误来源时，就该往后者演进——而这要靠评测数据来回答。

4. 什么时候值得做模板治理？
治理成本不低：逐段提规则、做控件、写批注，模板一升版还要跟着更新。我的判断标准很简单——**审稿人会不会逐句问”这句从哪来”**：

情况 建议
受控模板 + 要过伦理或监管（ICF、临床研究报告、申报资料） 值得完整治理
有模板，但内部使用、错了代价小 轻度治理：标出待填字段、写一页规则说明
没有固定模板，重在分析和观点 不需要模板治理，重点是证据和推理链

5. 机器通过 ≠ 可以直接用。
“0 错误”只代表结构正确。我们刻意让产品定位为”起草助手，不是定稿人”：出稿和质量分开表达，”草稿已生成”和”还有若干问题待处理”可以同时成立；定稿和签字永远是人。

八、可以迁移的方法

这个项目让我总结出一套适用于”受控模板 + 来源文档 → 合规草稿“这类问题的方法，可以迁移到分中心版 ICF、临床研究报告、方案摘要等场景：

  1. 模板和生成分开：模板离线治理、评审、发布；生成只用已发布的版本。
  2. 把暗规则写成明规则：颜色、括号、”如适用”这些靠人理解的约定，都改写成显式标签。
  3. 有限的规则类型 × 三种处理结果：固定 / 多选一 / 看条件 / 重复，每块只有”写入 / 删除 / 保留待确认”三种结果。
  4. AI 交结构化答案，程序负责排版和检查：格式和完整性交给确定性的程序。
  5. 每个填入值都带出处：这是让审稿人敢用的前提。
  6. **交付物是”可核对的草稿”**：修订痕迹、批注和核对清单，比文字本身更重要。

九、还没解决的问题

  • 一个方案对应多份 ICF：有的研究分好几个部分，各自需要一份 ICF，目前只能生成一份；
  • 配套文件和分中心版：怀孕伴侣授权书、分中心专用版等还没支持；
  • 评测数据：评测方案已经设计好，正在用已定稿项目跑第一版数据，后续会补上结果。

结语

做这个项目最大的收获，是重新理解了”AI 写文档”这件事。

在受控、高风险的场景里，AI 的价值不在于写得多好，而在于把人从”搬运和查找”里解放出来，同时把每一个不确定都摆到人面前。 一份让审稿人十分钟就知道该看哪里的草稿，比一份读起来很完美、却不知道哪里可能有错的终稿，有用得多。

而产品经理在这里的工作，与其说是设计功能，不如说是**设计”人和 AI 之间的分工边界”**：哪些交给模型判断，哪些交给程序保证，哪些必须留给人。

Before we start

The project was about using AI to draft informed consent forms (ICFs) for clinical trials.

Going in, I thought the problem was “have an LLM write a document by following a template.” Coming out, I found that the real difficulty had nothing to do with writing. It came down to three things:

  1. When the AI is unsure, will it honestly say “I don’t know” instead of inventing a sentence that sounds right?
  2. Can reviewers verify the draft quickly, instead of rereading it word by word?
  3. When the template is upgraded, can the system keep up?

The article follows the order problem → judgment → solution → deliverable → what I did → reflections. If you only have two minutes, read sections 2 and 7.

1. What an ICF is, and why it is hard to write

An informed consent form is the document a patient must read and sign before joining a clinical trial. Without a signature, no study procedure can begin; without the hospital ethics committee’s approval of the form, the study cannot enroll anyone.

Its relationship to the study protocol is roughly this:

  • The protocol is a “technical manual” for physicians and regulators: hundreds of pages of specialist language.
  • The ICF picks out only the parts that matter to the patient and explains them in plain words: which tests they will have, what the risks and benefits are, whether it costs anything, how their privacy is protected, and that they can withdraw at any time.

A typical ICF has a dozen or so sections, each answering one question a patient might ask:

Section The patient’s question Source
About the study / study drug What drug is being studied? How are groups assigned? Protocol, investigator’s brochure
What happens during the study What do I do at each visit? Visit schedule in the protocol
Risks and discomforts / pregnancy and contraception What are the risks? How long must I use contraception? Standard wording from pharmacovigilance, protocol
Benefits / alternative treatments What do I gain? What else could I do? Protocol, clinical team
Costs, privacy, samples Does it cost money? Where do my data and samples go? Budget, site information, regulations
Withdrawal, contacts, signature page Can I leave? Whom do I ask? Fixed wording, hospital-specific details

Drafting an ICF usually means a clinical operations colleague works through a protocol of several hundred pages and a company template of several dozen, section by section — deleting options, filling blanks, rewriting in plain language — and then sends it back and forth for review by pharmacovigilance, medical, legal and other functions.

It is hard in four ways:

  • Scattered information: visits, test items, contraception duration and prohibited medicines are spread across the protocol, many hidden in the footnotes of large tables.
  • Many options: the same paragraph often has several versions in the template (the study purpose alone has versions for phase I, active comparator, background therapy and so on), and the right one depends on the study design.
  • Translation into plain language: the protocol uses technical terms; the patient has to understand.
  • Errors carry compliance risk: writing “approved” without evidence, altering standard risk wording, or leaving out a required disclosure about cross-border data transfer can all get the form sent back by the ethics committee.

AI is good at the first three. The fourth is exactly where AI is most dangerous. The whole product design is about using the former and guarding against the latter.

2. The first key judgment: this is not a writing problem

The most obvious approach early on was to give the LLM both the protocol and the template and ask it to “write one following the template.”

The resulting drafts read smoothly, but the problems were all hidden:

  1. What should be deleted wasn’t, and what shouldn’t be was. Many company templates use font colour to mark what may be edited (black text must stay, blue text can change), but the blue text mixes blanks to fill, writing hints, option labels and candidate wording. A person gets it at a glance; the model can’t tell them apart.
  2. It invents when unsure. When it can’t find an approval number, a reimbursement amount or a contraception period, the model tends to fill in a plausible-looking value.
  3. Fixed wording gets polished. Clauses that regulations require to stay word-for-word get “smoothed out” by the model.
  4. Formatting doesn’t survive. Footer version numbers, numbering, and keeping the signature page on one page are hard to keep stable when the model edits Word directly.
  5. Reviewers can’t verify. They can’t tell which sentence came from the template, which the AI wrote, or where in the protocol the evidence is — so they reread everything, and the time saved is spent again.

So my definition of the problem became:

Drafting an ICF is not open-ended writing. It is making compliance decisions on a controlled template, plus pulling evidence from the protocol to fill in blanks.

That definition shaped everything that followed: the AI does not write freely; it answers multiple-choice and fill-in-the-blank questions within an explicit set of rules.

3. The solution: govern the template first, then generate

Since the template’s rules were “implicit rules that rely on human understanding,” the first step was to turn them into explicit rules a machine can execute. We called this step “template governance,” and it had three parts.

1. Give every block of the template an “address”

The whole template was cut into about 190 small blocks, each wrapped in a Word content control (a named box), named hierarchically as chapter → paragraph → option, for example:

The rule for cutting was: any meaning whose “keep or drop” can be judged on its own is one block. For instance, the male contraception part is split into five blocks — “effects on sperm,” “contraception requirements,” “no sperm donation,” “vasectomy exemption” and “partner pregnancy” — because different studies may need only some of them.

2. Write one processing rule for every block

Rules are attached to each box as Word comments. They boil down to four types:

Rule type In plain words Example
Always keep Do not change a word Signature page, study-injury clause
Pick one Keep exactly one of several candidates; each candidate states when it applies The four versions of the study purpose
Keep if condition met Keep only when the condition holds; if you can’t judge, keep it and say why Keep the MRI risk text only if “the study includes MRI”
Repeat per actual count The template gives one example; write as many as the protocol actually has Treatment groups, visit list

3. Mark blanks and writing hints inside each block

  • [field name] is a blank. Fill it if the protocol has the information; if not, leave it as is, so reviewers can see at a glance what’s missing.
  • {…} is a hint for the AI and is removed after generation.

These hints capture a great deal of business experience. A few typical ones:

  • If no approval number can be found, never write “approved,” and never substitute ethics approval for regulatory approval.
  • Oncology-specific phrases such as “subsequent anti-cancer therapy” must not appear in non-oncology studies.
  • Single-blind studies must not reuse the double-blind explanation.
  • Drug risks may only use standard wording supplied by pharmacovigilance, never AI-written text; but facts the protocol already states (such as which visit includes a pregnancy test) must not be left blank on the grounds that “pharmacovigilance needs to confirm.”

A complete example — the “blinding” block:

Part Content
Text “Neither you nor your study doctor will know in advance which group you are assigned to…”
Rule Keep if condition met: keep only if the protocol is double-blind; if you can’t judge, keep it and say why
Hint Single-blind or other designs cannot use this paragraph as is; write it according to the protocol

After reading the protocol, the AI can reach only three outcomes: double-blind → keep; open-label → delete; protocol unclear → keep and mark “to be confirmed.” All 190 or so blocks are handled the same way.

4. How the system runs: the AI hands in an “answer sheet,” the program handles layout and checks

I often explain it to business colleagues with an analogy:

  • Template = exam paper: about 190 questions, each with its own answering rules.
  • AI = examinee: after reading the protocol, it answers every question — keep or drop, which option, what to fill in, where the evidence is in the protocol, and why it is unsure.
  • Program = grading machine + typesetter: it first checks the answer sheet for missing questions and format errors, then puts each answer back into the template by its address and produces the Word file.

The full flow:

  1. Upload the protocol and choose the template.
  2. The system parses the protocol into structured text.
  3. Three groups draft in parallel: cover through study procedures, risks through costs, privacy through the signature page; each group self-checks when done.
  4. The three results are merged, then a full-document check runs once more.
  5. The program puts the answers back into the template by address and outputs a Word draft.

Two design trade-offs here:

Approach Benefit Cost
Draft by chapter groups in parallel Each group sees only part of the material, so it is more focused and faster; if one group fails, only that group reruns Groups may contradict each other, so the full-document check after merging is the safety net
AI only hands in answers, never edits Word directly The program guarantees formatting; answers are structured, so gaps can be checked automatically An answer format and a set of checking rules must be maintained

One principle runs through everything: when unsure, keep it and state the reason. The two kinds of error cost very different amounts — an extra paragraph takes a reviewer a few seconds to delete; a missing risk disclosure is a compliance problem.

5. Output design: telling reviewers where to look first

At this point I realized that the real deliverable of this kind of AI product is not “a final document” but “a draft that can be verified quickly.” So the output got a lot of attention.

A results summary in the conversation, with three things:

  1. The AI’s key judgment about the study, such as “a multicentre, single-arm, open-label phase III study.” This line should be checked first — if it is wrong, many keep-or-drop decisions downstream will be wrong too.
  2. The categories of information still needed: approval number, investigators and contacts at each hospital, standard drug-risk wording, reimbursement amounts, sample and cross-border details, and so on.
  3. A set of counts that is essentially a verification workload list:
Category Does the user need to act?
Blocks kept with confidence (mostly fixed text) Usually not
Kept blocks / control blocks needing attention Yes, review
Deleted blocks Review first — a wrong deletion means a missing disclosure
Fields still to fill Yes, fill in

The Word draft carries three layers of verification cues:

  • Tracked changes: content the AI removed doesn’t simply vanish; it appears as a strikethrough deletion, which the reviewer accepts or rejects.
  • Highlights: mark content that needs attention and fields still to fill.
  • Comments: every block has a comment in a fixed format — the action taken, which block it is, which rule was triggered, the evidence or the reason for uncertainty, and any missing fields.

The review order we recommend: check the study-type judgment first → then the deletions → then blocks needing attention → finally fill in fields. Highest risk to lowest.

6. What I did on the project

After taking over the project, my main contributions were:

1. Understanding the rule system and translating it into business language. I read every block’s rules and hints in the template, grouped them into the four rule types and their distribution, and wrote a project explainer that needs no technical background, so business, pharmacovigilance and engineering could discuss issues in the same language.

2. Finding and pinpointing a template-version mismatch. The company template was upgraded mid-project. Comparing the old and new versions paragraph by paragraph, I found the chapter structure unchanged; the real changes fell into three groups:

  • Standardized wording (word changes only);
  • A new disclosure paragraph on “transferring personal information and biological samples abroad” (kept only when the study involves cross-border transfer);
  • New reminders that “companion documents must be generated separately” (such as a pregnant partner authorization form and a personal information processing notice).

The system’s base template was still the old version. Reviewers would catch the first group; the second group was a real compliance gap for any project involving cross-border transfer. As a result I pushed to bind “template version → rule version → base template version” together: whenever the template is upgraded, the rules must be updated first, then the base template, then a trial run on a project that exercises the change.

3. Diagnosing “too many items to confirm.” A draft often had dozens of items needing human confirmation. I split them into two kinds:

  • Information genuinely missing: the protocol simply doesn’t contain it (contacts at each hospital, reimbursement amounts). The fix is an upload entry for supplementary materials, with items routed by owner (pharmacovigilance / legal / budget / site).
  • In the protocol, but the AI didn’t find it: this is what needs optimizing, and evaluation is needed to pinpoint which kinds of information and which table formats parse poorly.

4. Designing the evaluation. “Zero errors” from the program’s check only means the answer sheet is complete, not that the answers are right. The evaluation I designed:

  • Uses finalized ICFs that have passed ethics review as the gold standard, covering phase I / phase III, oncology / non-oncology, double-blind / open-label, healthy volunteers / patients;
  • Maps each finalized ICF back to the template block by block and compares it with the AI’s answers;
  • Tracks a few numbers: keep/drop accuracy, fill-in recall and precision, visit coverage, plus two red lines: zero fabricated values and zero changes to standard wording;
  • Asks human reviewers not for a 1–5 score but three questions per block: Are the facts right? Should it be there? Is it understandable? That way every problem traces directly to a block’s rule, prompt or parsing.

5. Removing technical jargon from the experience. The progress cards originally showed prompts, script commands and file paths, and comments used internal notation like “COVER/L4” and “always.” For business users, all of that should become result language such as “Group 1 of 3 done, 0 errors, 12 items to confirm.”

7. Trade-offs and reflections

1. Where should the rules live: in the Word template, or in code and prompts?
We chose the Word template (as comments). The cost is that engineers find it less convenient to change; the benefit is that the business side can read the rules and help maintain them. For controlled documents, ownership of the rules belongs with the business side, and that matters more than engineering convenience.

2. When unsure, keep or delete?
We chose “keep and explain why.” The cost is more items to confirm, so the checklist experience has to be good — follow-on work that this design choice inevitably creates.

3. Split by chapter, or by type of fact?
We discussed two architectures. One drafts chapter groups in parallel (fast and simple). The other first extracts facts shared across chapters — the current study drug, key design decisions, the full visit matrix, risks — into a few “fact sheets,” then writes them into every chapter (more consistent, but slower). We currently use the first and rely on the full-document check for consistency. My view: once contradictions across chapters become the main source of errors, it’s time to move to the second — and only evaluation data can tell us when.

4. When is template governance worth it?
Governance isn’t cheap: extracting rules paragraph by paragraph, building controls, writing comments, and updating everything whenever the template changes. My test is simple — will reviewers ask “where did this sentence come from?” line by line?

Situation Recommendation
Controlled template + ethics or regulatory review (ICFs, clinical study reports, submission documents) Worth full governance
Template exists, but internal use and low cost of errors Light governance: mark fields to fill and write a one-page rule guide
No fixed template; the value is in analysis and argument No template governance needed; focus on evidence and the reasoning chain

5. Passing the machine check ≠ ready to use.
“Zero errors” only means the structure is correct. We deliberately positioned the product as “a drafting assistant, not the finalizer”: generation and quality are reported separately, “draft generated” and “several issues still open” can both be true, and finalizing and signing always belong to people.

8. A transferable method

The project gave me a method for problems of the form “controlled template + source documents → compliant draft,” which carries over to site-specific ICFs, clinical study reports, protocol synopses and similar documents:

  1. Separate the template from generation: govern, review and publish the template offline; generation only uses published versions.
  2. Turn implicit rules into explicit ones: conventions like colour, brackets and “if applicable” all become explicit labels.
  3. A small set of rule types × three outcomes: always keep / pick one / keep if condition met / repeat, and every block ends in one of three outcomes — write, delete, or keep pending confirmation.
  4. The AI submits structured answers; the program handles layout and checks: formatting and completeness go to deterministic code.
  5. Every filled-in value carries its source: this is what makes reviewers willing to trust it.
  6. The deliverable is a verifiable draft: tracked changes, comments and the review checklist matter more than the prose itself.

9. Open problems

  • One protocol, several ICFs: some studies have several parts, each needing its own ICF; today only one can be generated.
  • Companion documents and site-specific versions: pregnant partner authorization forms, site-specific versions and the like are not yet supported.
  • Evaluation data: the evaluation design is ready, and the first round is running on finalized projects; results will be added later.

Closing

The biggest lesson from this project was a new understanding of what “AI writing documents” means.

In controlled, high-stakes settings, AI’s value is not in how well it writes, but in freeing people from moving and searching for information while putting every uncertainty in front of them. A draft that tells a reviewer in ten minutes where to look is far more useful than a final document that reads perfectly but hides where it might be wrong.

And the product manager’s job here is less about designing features than about designing the division of labour between people and AI: what the model decides, what the program guarantees, and what must stay with people.
